🦴 Ankle radiography

Standard ankle series

APMortiseLateral

Use the department's local protocol and referral/clinical information. The routine series is designed to assess the distal tibia and fibula, talus, ankle mortise and adjacent soft tissues.

Core principle: centre and collimate to the ankle, include the relevant distal tibia/fibula and soft tissues, and obtain diagnostic images with minimal rotation and motion.

Patient preparation

  • Confirm identity, examination and side; check the clinical history and mechanism of injury.
  • Remove external artefacts where practical.
  • Assess pain, mobility and whether the patient can safely stand or rotate the limb.
  • Use appropriate immobilisation/support in trauma rather than forcing the injured ankle into a position.

Common indications

  • Acute trauma, pain, swelling or suspected fracture/dislocation.
  • Follow-up of known fracture or post-reduction assessment where requested.
  • Persistent symptoms or other clinical indications according to local referral criteria.

🚨 Ankle trauma
Trauma principle: prioritise patient safety and the local trauma pathway. Do not force a painful or unstable ankle into a position simply to achieve a standard projection.

Before positioning

MechanismTwist, fall, direct impact, sports injury or high-energy trauma.
Clinical siteDocument pain, swelling, deformity and the point(s) of tenderness where available.
Neurovascular statusEscalate according to local trauma policy if there are concerning neurovascular findings.
ImmobilisationMaintain immobilisation where clinically required; adapt positioning rather than forcing movement.

Projection strategy

APInclude the ankle joint and lower third of tibia/fibula.
MortiseObtain the requested rotation only as tolerated and according to local protocol.

📋 Ottawa ankle rules
Decision-support only: the Ottawa rules are a clinical decision aid and do not replace clinical assessment or local imaging policy.

When ankle radiography is indicated

For traumatic ankle pain in the malleolar zone, RCR describes the key Ottawa criteria as tenderness at the posterior edge/tip of the lateral or medial malleolus, or inability to bear weight both immediately after injury and in the ED for four steps. citeturn0search1

AP ankle

Positioning

  • Patient supine or seated; leg extended as tolerated.
  • Place the ankle in a true AP position as far as pain permits; avoid unnecessary rotation.
  • Foot should be approximately perpendicular to the detector when tolerated.

Central ray & collimation

  • Centre to the ankle joint / midway between the malleoli according to local protocol.
  • Collimate to include the ankle joint, distal tibia/fibula and soft tissues.
  • Use the department's approved exposure factors and detector protocol rather than a fixed value from an app.

Image criteria

  • Ankle joint demonstrated without excessive rotation.
  • Distal tibia and fibula, talus and surrounding soft tissues included.
  • Medial and lateral malleoli visualised adequately; joint relationship assessed with the other views.
Mortise view

Positioning

  • Supine or seated with the leg supported.
  • Internally rotate the entire leg/foot as required by your department to open the ankle mortise; a commonly taught position is around 15–20° internal rotation.
  • Do not force rotation in acute trauma; stop if pain or instability prevents safe positioning.

Central ray & collimation

  • Centre to the ankle joint / midway between the malleoli according to local protocol.
  • Collimate tightly to the ankle region while retaining the required anatomy.

Image criteria

  • Mortise should be well demonstrated with the talar dome and tibial plafond visible.
  • Medial, lateral and superior joint spaces should be assessable.
  • Distal tibiofibular relationship should be visible.
Why mortise? It reduces the overlap created by the AP view and improves assessment of the ankle joint space and distal tibiofibular relationship.
Lateral ankle

Positioning

  • Patient lateral or semi-lateral as tolerated; affected side closest to detector.
  • Flex the knee if needed for comfort and stability.
  • Keep the ankle in a true lateral position as closely as pain permits.

Central ray & collimation

  • Centre to the ankle joint according to local protocol.
  • Include distal tibia/fibula, talus, calcaneus as required for the ankle study and adjacent soft tissues.

Image criteria

  • Talus and ankle joint shown in lateral profile.
  • Distal tibia and fibula adequately included.
  • Minimal unwanted rotation; soft-tissue swelling and posterior malleolar region assessed.
Trauma modifications
Safety first: Do not force a painful or potentially unstable ankle into routine positioning. Adapt the technique to the patient's injury, immobilisation and clinical condition.

Before imaging

  • Check for deformity, open injury, severe swelling and neurovascular concerns.
  • Maintain immobilisation when indicated and follow local trauma/orthopaedic instructions.
  • Move the patient as little as necessary; use horizontal-beam or other modified positioning when required by local protocol.

Think beyond the ankle

  • Consider whether the clinical information suggests injury extending into the distal tibia/fibula or foot.
  • Look at the proximal fibula when the mechanism and tenderness raise concern for a more extensive injury.
  • Consider additional views or cross-sectional imaging when requested/clinically indicated.
Do not miss: obvious malalignment, dislocation, displaced fracture, widening/asymmetry of the mortise, or a suspicious subtle fracture line should trigger appropriate clinical escalation and/or additional imaging according to local practice.

Systematic review

  • Distal tibia: plafond, malleolus, cortical continuity.
  • Fibula: lateral malleolus and visible distal shaft.
  • Talus: dome, neck/body and alignment.
  • Mortise: medial/lateral/superior joint spaces and distal tibiofibular relationship.
  • Calcaneus / foot: inspect visible portions for an obvious associated injury.
  • Soft tissues: swelling, effusion indicators, gas or foreign body where relevant.

🦶 Ottawa Ankle Rules

When ankle radiographs may be indicated

For adults and children over 5 years in acute ankle trauma, the Ottawa Ankle Rules are a clinical decision aid used to determine whether radiography is required.

Radiographs are indicated when:
  • Inability to bear weight both immediately after injury and in the emergency/clinical assessment, or
  • Bone tenderness at the posterior edge/inferior tip of either malleolus, or
  • Inability to walk four steps.

Follow the current local/NHS protocol. Do not apply the rule in patients who fall outside its validated/exclusion criteria, such as very young children or patients whose neurological status/sensation or communication makes the assessment unreliable.

Clinical reminder

The Ottawa rules help decide whether imaging is needed; they do not replace clinical assessment, examination of the whole injured limb, or departmental protocols.

🚨 Ankle trauma workflow

Quick workflow
  1. Confirm identity, side and mechanism.
  2. Check referral information and clinical decision criteria.
  3. Assess pain, deformity, immobilisation and ability to move safely.
  4. Apply local imaging criteria / Ottawa rules where appropriate.
  5. Perform AP, mortise and lateral views when indicated, adapting positioning to the injury.
  6. Evaluate alignment, mortise, cortical continuity and soft tissues.
  7. Escalate/add views or imaging according to local protocol and clinical need.
